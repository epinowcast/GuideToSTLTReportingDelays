STLT Delays Guide

Accounting for Reporting Delays in Public Health Surveillance

Public health surveillance data are rarely complete when first reported. Cases, hospitalisations, and other events take time to be confirmed, entered into databases, and reported to central systems. These reporting delays mean that the most recent data systematically undercount the true number of events, complicating real-time analysis.

Recent reported values can therefore give a misleading picture of what is happening now, especially when reporting is severely incomplete. Our goal is to help public health teams estimate the values that recent data are likely to reach once more reports arrive and understand how uncertain those estimates are. This can help teams avoid mistaking reporting delays for real changes in disease activity and support better situational awareness.

Several approaches can help public health teams work towards this goal. This guide introduces the available options and focuses on practical guidance for choosing and implementing methods in STLT settings, with emphasis on adoption, communication, and real-world case studies. It is intended as a companion to a technical overview of nowcasting methods that covers mathematical formulation and computational properties in detail.

Who is this for?

  • Epidemiologists and other state, tribal, local, and territorial (STLT) public health practitioners in the United States working with delayed surveillance data and other real-time data issues.
  • Modellers and analysts expanding their understanding of reporting delays and the methods available to address them.
  • Staff in healthcare facilities, laboratories, and other reporting organisations seeking to understand how reporting processes affect downstream surveillance analyses.

What this guide covers

  • Challenges that arise when using surveillance data in real time, including reporting delays, data revisions, and site drop-in and drop-out.
  • Modelling approaches that can adjust for reporting delays, from chain-ladder approaches to Bayesian hierarchical models.
  • A decision tree to help select an appropriate method based on your data characteristics and use case.
  • A how-to guide covering the common steps from assessing data through to communicating results.
  • Case studies demonstrating practical implementations in surveillance settings.
